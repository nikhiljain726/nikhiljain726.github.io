The Art Galore journey, from zero to 3 crore to shutdown

D2C & founder lessons  ·  Mar 2026  ·  7 min read

I ran a personalized-products brand called Art Galore from 2020 to 2022. It grew fast, and then I closed it. Here is the whole arc, the good and the parts that ended it, because the honest version is more useful than the highlight reel.

The problem I started with

I started in drop-shipping, and the problem that pulled me in was personalized gifting. A personalized gift is genuinely hard to get right, because personalization is personal. People have a picture in their head and they want exactly that thing, and almost nobody was nailing it. That was the gap on the buyer side. On the supply side there were hundreds of creators who could make these products and no marketplace built for them, no Amazon for personalized work. Art Galore was my answer to both.

Founding and the early days

I bootstrapped it, solo, from Gurugram. No co-founder, no outside money, so the plan was simply to sell something real and let revenue fund the next step. The early challenge was doing everything at once: acquiring customers, building the product, and standing up the operations to actually make and ship personalized items on time. I leaned on what I knew, which was performance marketing, and figured out the rest as it broke.

From zero to 3 crore

In the first year we crossed 3 crore in revenue and 14,000-plus customers, almost entirely through Meta and Google. Two decisions did the heavy lifting. First, I ran the entire customer journey on WhatsApp: discovery, order notifications, design validation, and post-purchase. For a personalized product, design validation is the tense moment, and doing it as a conversation instead of a form is what kept conversion alive. Second, I built a no-code vendor portal so creators could manage orders and confirm designs directly with customers while their phone numbers stayed masked. That let me add supply without adding risk. I set up an 1800 square foot unit and a team of ten to keep production moving.

Selling faster than you can make is not growth. It is a backlog with a refund attached.

Setbacks, and the two things that closed it

The category was trend-driven, and I could not keep pace with the product side. Personalized gifting turns over fast, almost like a daily-objects business, and I kept planning capacity for what was selling now instead of what was coming next. I was good at acquiring customers and slow at reading where taste was heading, and that gap widened as we grew.

The other constraint was structural. I could not find a co-founder, and without one it was far harder to raise. It can be done alone, but solo fundraising for a bootstrapped D2C brand in that category is an uphill climb, and without capital I could not build the product and supply muscle fast enough to stay ahead of the trend cycle. Those two things, product pace and no co-founder to unlock funding, are what led me to shut it down rather than limp along.

What I would do differently

Two lessons I carry into everything since. In a trend-driven category, resource and product planning is a forecasting problem, not a fulfilment one, and I treated it as fulfilment for too long. And find the co-founder before you need the money, because the hardest parts of building are much harder to do alone.

For anyone starting: start with something real that earns repeat purchases without push notifications, run the relationship where the customer already is, and automate the boring middle so a small team can hold a large operation. Art Galore did not last, but every one of those habits is still how I work.

Milestones along the way

The workshop, Tikamgarh, MP. We hit good traction early and could not keep up with demand, so instead of staying asset-light the way investors like, we set up an 1,800 square foot unit for in-house manufacturing. We had no experience with 3D printing, and making finished products felt far-fetched. The only thing that made it work was refusing to accept that it could not be done. We started with a couple of 3D printers, then added a big laser-engraving setup. Going through the pain of making personalized products firsthand is exactly what later gave me an edge in understanding sellers with the same problem. Do it yourself before you tell anyone else how.

The pitch. Art Galore was picked as one of five startups to pitch at 91springboard Gurugram, organized by 100X.VC. It was our first offline pitch after a run of remote ones, standing in front of the panel and taking the feedback live. A different kind of nervous, and a good one.
