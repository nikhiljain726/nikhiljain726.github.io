Medkart, the road ahead: a growth teardown

A plan I wrote for a regional generic-medicine chain: how it becomes a national D2C brand, from content to three-hour delivery.

D2C & founder lessons  ·  Nov 2022  ·  6 min read

A while back I got to dig into Medkart Pharmacy and write up how I would grow it. Medkart sells generic medicines at a fraction of branded prices through an omnichannel model, with more than 100 offline stores concentrated in Gujarat and a foot in the clinical lab market. The prize is obvious. The market for medicine is enormous and most of it is still branded. Convert even a slice of that to generic and you have a national business. This is the plan I laid out.

Two things stood in the way:

  1. D2C was a small share of the business, and it needed to grow fast over the next two to three years.
  2. The growth engine was franchise, built store by store. That is slow, and for going national, time is the scarce resource.

Two signals said the core was healthy. Chronic patients repeat at a very high rate, and the brand is strong in and around Ahmedabad. So the job was not to fix the model. It was to widen it.

Growing the D2C contribution

  1. Content and SEO. Build the encyclopedia of generic medicines. Make people aware, educate them, earn trust, and the sales follow. It is how 1mg, PharmEasy, and Practo grew, and Medkart can do it again for generics. English search is a crowded fight, but the bigger players have barely touched regional-language content, and that is where Medkart should aim to be number one. Add an in-house person making short video and reels to put a face to the brand. Content and SEO are the slowest to start and the highest return over time.
  2. Brand play. Medkart sits in a brand-play category, so build top-of-mind awareness across TV, print, out-of-home, select partnerships, and digital. Choose channels honestly, on three questions: is the audience actually relevant, what does a reach or impression cost here, and can you measure it. A print run in the Times of India NCR edition, for example, is relevant if it sits in the health section, fairly priced, and measurable if you make it so, a QR that opens a WhatsApp message, or a lift in branded search. Start small on each channel, watch the numbers, and double down on the winners. Carry one USP across everything, and if you do not know it yet, test and back your read with data.
  3. Performance marketing. Across digital, email, WhatsApp, push, and SMS, this is what pulls someone from the top of the funnel to a first purchase once the brand has made contact. It is less about finding brand-new users and more about converting the ones already aware. Pair it with telesales for high-value orders and it becomes a real driver of growth.
  4. Phased offline to support online. Nothing builds trust like a storefront. Put key outlets in high-footfall parts of the metros, not for coverage but for the consumer connection. Or go all-in on one city, prove the model, and scale it hard rather than crawling city by city.
  5. Product innovation. Get obsessive about the journey. Medkart should have the highest conversion rate in the category, because it offers the most value, so the basics have to be flawless: an accurate delivery date, clear descriptions, a clean add-to-cart, and the whole journey married to WhatsApp. Then replicate the offline behaviour online. Upload a prescription, get a quote for the generic equivalent, all in a WhatsApp thread. That flow could be the real unlock for acquisition.

The backend drivers

None of the front-end works without the back end:

  1. A strong, efficient supply chain, so selection and availability hold up.
  2. Customer support that answers every query within four hours. That one pledge alone can double repeat rates.
  3. Delivery speed. Aim for three-hour delivery in every metro, and treat sub-day delivery as the floor, whether through a quick-commerce partnership or a Domino's-style own network. In five years, waiting more than a day for medicine will feel absurd.
The market for medicine is enormous, and most of it is still branded. The opportunity is to take a share of that and convert it to generic.

So the move is not to boil the ocean. Run a pilot in one region, standardize the D2C playbook, raise the round to fund it, and go become the number one brand in generic medicine, passing real savings to millions of people. Reading it back, the opportunity was hard to miss.
