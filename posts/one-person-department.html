High-impact individual contributors: how AI is reshaping work

The management ladder was the only way up. AI just built a second staircase. On the HI-C, the data under it, and the systems that make one person a department.

Future of work  ·  Aug 2026  ·  8 min read

For most of the last decade there was one way to grow a career: manage more people. I stopped believing that a while ago, and I have spent the last few years building the opposite. There is a name for it now, the High-impact Individual Contributor, the HI-C: a senior person with no direct reports who carries a project from an idea to a number that actually moves, on their own. It is the direction I have been building toward the whole time, and the rest of the market is catching up fast, with the trade press calling it AI turning solo workers into departments and an army of one. Different names, one idea.

The ladder we were sold

For most of my career the deal was simple. Impact meant headcount. The way to matter more was to manage more people, and org chart altitude was the score. I have sat through enough hiring conversations to know the opening question is usually the same: how many people do you manage. Not what you shipped, not the problem you solved, not the budget. Just how many sat under you.

The quiet damage in that system is that it promotes people out of the thing they are good at. Someone with real craft gets pulled off the craft to coordinate other people doing it, and is then told that going back into the work is micromanaging. We take our best builders and hand them a calendar. That made sense when building was slow and someone had to sit in the middle to review, approve, and pass information up and down. It makes a lot less sense now.

The 2026 enlightenment ladder
Climb to manager, to have impact through other people.
Climb to director, to have impact through other managers.
Climb to VP, so the org chart says you matter.
Stay an IC, wire up AI, and ship the whole thing yourself.

Why this is not just a LinkedIn phase

The easy move is to roll your eyes at another future-of-work label. But the numbers under this one are specific. In a 2026 METR survey, technical workers put the value AI added to their work at roughly double a year earlier. Research out of Stanford and Wharton found AI explains a large share of the variation in what one person produces, from a fifth to well over half, and almost none of the variation at the team level. Read that twice. AI makes an individual much better and a group only a little.

At the same time Gallup has manager engagement falling, to the point that the people doing the work are now often more engaged than the people coordinating it. The org chart we inherited was built for a world where building was slow, so we paid layers of people to review, approve, and carry information around. When trying something is cheaper than the meeting to debate it, those layers stop being safety and start being drag.

AI does not make the group smarter. It makes the individual fast enough that the coordination layer becomes the bottleneck.

Escaping the coordination loop

Here is the shape of the old system. An idea starts with an IC, travels up to a manager, then a director, then a VP for a decision, then back down again before anything gets built. Every hop is a wait. As a company grows, the rows stack until a lot of the org is coordinating other people's coordination. When you can build and test a thing in an afternoon, most of that relay is pure latency. The cost of a wrong guess dropped, so the case for a chain of people whose job is to de-risk the guess dropped with it.

Average is the unlock, not genius

The thing that makes this work now is not that AI is brilliant. The sharpest framing I have heard for it is average intelligence, a phrase from Ravi Mehta. AI is automatically competent, at a median professional level, across design, copy, code, and analysis. Not great, not differentiated, just reliably average. That sounds like faint praise until you notice what it does for a senior person. If you are already good at one craft, average-but-instant help on the four functions next to it is enough to get a real thing into the world without staffing a team first. You build, you pressure-test, you validate, and only then do you pull in a true expert once there is momentum worth the pull.

I have been living the small version of this

My whole pitch, GTM engineering, is the HI-C idea aimed at go-to-market. I do not run a RevOps team, an SDR pod, and a data desk. I build the systems that do their repeatable parts, then I run the loop end to end. The open-source CRM server I shipped gives any AI client full read and write access to the pipeline. A next-best-step agent reads each deal and writes the action, with a reason, straight into the CRM as a task. Another watches resolved website visits and routes the warm ones to a rep the same day. Company research runs to a fixed schema with a source on every field. None of that is a team. It is one operator plus tooling doing work that used to take five.

My line for it has not changed: GTM engineering is mostly refusing to do by hand what a system can do reliably. Which is why, when the choice shows up, it is not really a choice.

GTM engineering, every single time
Hire another headcount
Build a system that does it
One of these has a monthly invoice. The other compounds.

The part the headlines skip

What people are actually arguing about is worth repeating honestly, because it is not all upside. On one side, a lot of operators are relieved. The management track was the only way up, and plenty of good builders took it and lost the craft that got them there. Ask around and operators say the quiet part out loud: the VP title has stopped being the flex. On the other side, two real objections keep coming up. The first is human. You can automate the org chart down to one person, but you cannot automate that person's need for a second opinion, a sounding board, and someone to catch the thing they cannot see. Going solo carries a cost that never shows up in a productivity chart. The second is structural. This is thriving at small, AI-native companies precisely because the bureaucracy has not grown yet. Drop the same model into a large enterprise with compliance, hand-offs, and genuine cross-team coordination, and it strains. Even Forbes, making the bullish case, lands on that caveat.

What actually unlocks it: information access

Strip it down and the lever is not the model. Average intelligence is enough to staff the functions next to your craft. The real unlock is information access. A senior IC working with a junior's context cannot operate at a director's scope, no matter how sharp the tooling is. The companies where this works trust their people with the whole picture. The ones where it stalls have a middle layer whose value is controlling what everyone else is allowed to see, and that control is exactly what a HI-C role removes. It is the same thing I hit on the GTM side. The system only works when the signals, from the CRM, from LinkedIn, from the market, reach the person who can act, without a week of relay in between. A simple test for whether your org can handle this: hand a high-potential junior a full-stack project and watch where they get stuck. Wherever they hit a wall of context they are not allowed to have, that is your bottleneck.

The other half is comp. IC used to mean less money by definition, so nobody chose it. Break that link, pay for the outcome instead of the headcount, and suddenly recruiting a former VP into an IC seat is a real strategy rather than a demotion. When one person can carry department-level impact, the budget should follow the impact, which means it flows to the people doing the work.

So I am not treating the HI-C as a prediction. It is a fair description of the job I already do, and a bet I have been making with my own time. Get very good at one craft, wire up the average-but-good-enough functions around it, and own the outcome instead of the headcount. The title was never the point. The number that moves is.
