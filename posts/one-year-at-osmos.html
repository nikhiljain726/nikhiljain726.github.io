One year at Osmos

A year ago I joined to build the growth and GTM engineering function. What the year was, what I built, and what it taught me.

Notes  ·  Nov 2026  ·  6 min read
TL;DR

I joined to build go-to-market as a system. A year in, the outcomes are the point: organic went from a small share of leads to our largest source, every lead is captured and routed to a seller the same day, the CRM is clean after years of neglect, and AI assistants now cite us on nearly every buying question we track. The systems underneath, signal-to-action pipelines, a CRM rebuilt as infrastructure, targeted paid ads, personalized email, and a relaunched site, are what produced them. The lesson that stuck: the work is not the clever idea, it is the system that makes the clever idea happen every day without you.

A year ago I joined Osmos to build the growth and GTM engineering function close to scratch. Osmos builds the retail media infrastructure layer for marketplaces and retailers. My job was the other side of that coin: get our own go-to-market running like a system. This is the public version of my year-one reflection, the honest shape of it, with the internal numbers left where they belong.

Start with outcomes, because that is what the year is judged on. The shape of the before and after:

None of that was the goal in itself. The goal was a go-to-market that runs like a system: take the signals already lying around, from the website, the CRM, LinkedIn, and the market, turn them into something a person can act on the same day, and wire it so it keeps running. The rest of this is how.

What I built

Most of the pieces are written up here in more detail:

  • An open-source server that gives any AI client full read and write access to the CRM, with dry-runs and an audit log.
  • A next-best-step agent that reads each deal and writes the action, with a reason, as a task in the CRM.
  • A pipeline that turns anonymous website visits into same-day intent for a rep.
  • Competitor and intent tracking that turns attention into a target list with a reason to reach out.
  • Company research at scale, to a strict schema with a source on every field.
  • A real cleanup of a CRM nobody had touched in years, with a safety rig that made it safe to fix.
  • Multitouch attribution built on activities, so the CRM stops overwriting the story of how a deal came in.

It was not all internal plumbing. A good part of the year ran outward, with the same signal-first discipline:

  • Highly targeted paid ads, built around tight audiences and real intent rather than broad spray.
  • Personalized email campaigns at scale, each message tied to what the person actually did, not a blast to a list.
  • A full revamp and relaunch of the website.
  • SEO and AEO work, so we show up both in classic search and in the answers AI assistants hand back.
  • Launched the Osmos app for ChatGPT, so the product meets buyers inside the assistant they already use.

How it was built: AI-native, one operator

The thing I am proudest of is not any single pipeline. It is that nearly all of it was built AI-native, by one person working as a high-impact IC: the kind of work that usually spans a RevOps lead, a marketing operations engineer, and a data analyst, run instead by one operator with AI in the loop. Through a year of team changes, the function kept shipping on that model, one person doing the work of several.

AI-native does not mean hands-off. The rule I hold is simple: a person approves before anything changes in the CRM or goes live. My standing instruction to my own agents is "don't write, first show me." Every number is sourced, and I stand behind every word. The AI is leverage, not an excuse to skip the judgment.

What it taught me

A CRM is infrastructure, not a filing cabinet. An agent is only as good as what it can touch, which is why I spent so much of the year making the CRM something software could read and write safely, not just a place humans type into.

The hard part is never the signal, it is the last mile. There is no shortage of signals. What is rare is wiring one to a specific action, for a specific person, the same day it is still warm. A signal nobody acts on that day is just a log entry.

Build the system before you ask for the headcount. GTM engineering is mostly refusing to do by hand what a system can do reliably. The budget and the people follow the leverage, not the other way around.

How it changed how I work

I came in thinking of myself as a growth and marketing lead. I leave the year closer to an operator who builds. The role I keep reaching for is the one I wrote about, the high-impact IC: one person plus tooling doing the work of a team. That is not a flex about working alone. It is a bet that the leverage now sits with whoever can go from signal to shipped without a relay in the middle.

What year two looks like

More of the same shape, deeper. More of the loop automated, the systems compounding on each other, and the parts that still run by hand handed to the machine wherever it is reliable. The goal has not moved since day one: find what actually moves revenue, then build the thing that keeps it moving.

The work is not the clever idea. It is the system that makes the clever idea happen every day without you.

A year in, that is the lesson that stuck, and it is the least glamorous one. It is also the part I enjoy most.
