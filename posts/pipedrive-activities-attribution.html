Multitouch attribution in Pipedrive, built on activities

Custom fields overwrite the last touch and keep no timeline. Activities are timestamped, ownered, and unlimited. Here is how I turn every interaction into one.

GTM & growth engineering  ·  Aug 2026  ·  6 min read
TL;DR

A source custom field in Pipedrive holds one value, so the last automation to write wins and the earlier touch is lost. Fields keep no timeline; activities do. Activities are timestamped, ownered, linked to a person, organization, or deal, and unlimited on paid plans. So I log every meaningful interaction as an activity, with a custom activity type per touch, and the record grows a readable multitouch history. Keep a single source field for headline reporting, let the activity stream carry the journey, and automate the logging so it actually happens.

Here is a story that plays out in every CRM I have touched. Marketing runs the website, the landing pages, the lead forms, and a steady outbound motion. A deal comes in, lands in Pipedrive tagged with a source, say Inbound, and gets assigned to a seller. The seller opens it and says: wait, I know this company. I met them at an event last month, and I logged it back then. Now the record has two truths, the inbound touch and the event touch, and one field to hold them. Whatever wrote last wins. The earlier touch is simply gone.

Why a custom field cannot hold a journey

We tried the obvious fix first: a custom field where a rep tags the event where they met a company. It breaks for two reasons. The first is mechanical. An automation that stamps the source overwrites whatever the rep typed, because a custom field is a single slot. The second is deeper. A custom field stores a value, not a history. It has no timeline, so you cannot ask it what happened first. And you cannot patch that by adding Source 1, Source 2, Source 3, Touch 4, Touch 5 as separate fields. That sprawls immediately, it still has no order, and nobody hand-fills fifteen fields.

The reframe: interactions are events, so store them as events

Stop forcing a journey into a field. Record it as a series of events. In Pipedrive the object built for exactly that is the Activity. An activity is timestamped. It has an owner. It attaches to a person, or a person and an organization, or a person and an organization and a deal. And, quietly important, activities are unlimited on every paid plan, the same as notes, except that unlike notes they carry the owner, the time, and the links. So instead of overwriting a source, I append an activity for every interaction, and the record grows a real timeline you can read top to bottom.

How Pipedrive is wired

It helps to see the shape of the data. Organizations hold people, people sit on deals, and custom fields hang off each of those as flat, single-value slots. Activities are the one object with a clock: every activity has a time and links up to a person, an organization, or a deal. That is the difference the whole approach rests on.

How I set it up

The trick that makes the stream readable is a custom Activity Type for each kind of interaction. Pipedrive lets you define your own types, so I made one per touch: Website visit, Form fill, Content download, Email reply, Outbound email, LinkedIn touch, Event meeting, Webinar, Demo. Then every automation writes its interaction into Pipedrive as a dated activity of the right type, linked to the person and, where one exists, the deal. The visitor-identification tool logs a Website visit. The form handler logs a Form fill. The outbound sequence logs each send and reply. Reps log the offline touches by hand, like the event where they met someone. Nothing overwrites anything, because each touch is its own row.

The honest caveats

This approach has a cost, so a few caveats before you copy it. Activities are only as good as what writes them, which means the logging has to be automated. Lean on reps to remember and the timeline fills with holes. Be selective too. Auto-logging every page view buries the touches that matter, so I keep custom types and record the meaningful interactions rather than the noise. And reporting on activities is harder than reporting on one field, because Pipedrive dashboards lean on fields. The way I square that is to keep both. The stream holds the full history, and a small automation writes the first touch and the last touch back into two plain fields, so the pipeline reports stay simple while the journey stays intact.

The impact

Now the attribution is not a guess. You can read the order of touches straight off the record: the blog post pulled them in, the playbook download warmed them, the seller closed the loop at an event. Marketing and sales both get credit for the touch they actually contributed, because both touches are still there. Nothing gets overwritten, so you also get an audit trail for free, which matters the day someone asks why a deal is sourced the way it is. And it costs nothing extra: activities do not count against a field limit or a plan tier. First touch, last touch, and everything in between, on any version of Pipedrive.

Custom fields are for the current state of a record. The path that got it there is a sequence of events, so store it as events.

Model the interactions as activities and the CRM stops flattening your best story into a single overwritten field. The timeline becomes the attribution, and it is one every rep and marketer can actually read.
